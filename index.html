1 · The Problem

Friday, Diwali festival week. You are the Reservations Manager of Hotel Shri Shakti Grand, a 100-room medium hotel (Unit 2: medium = 26–100 lettable rooms). The General Manager asks: “How many rooms beyond 100 should we sell tonight?”

Some guests hold guaranteed reservations (deposit, credit card, company, airline, travel agent). Others hold non-guaranteed (6 PM) reservations. Guaranteed no-shows are charged, so the hotel keeps one day’s room charge; non-guaranteed no-shows pay nothing. If more guests arrive than rooms, someone is walked, which costs money and harms reputation at the most critical stage: Arrival.

7 · Instructor Solutions & Scenario Answer Guide

How to use: These are model answers and expected trends, not one fixed answer for every slider combination. The simulation is stochastic, so the exact best overbooking level and net contribution depend on the settings and simulated arrivals. Use the live chart and figures as evidence.

Core formula used by this page
Average net contribution per night = room revenue + guaranteed no-show charges retained − walking costs.
In the code: net = (rooms accommodated × ADR) + (guaranteed no-shows × ADR) − (guests walked × walking cost), averaged over 1,500 simulated nights.

Model answers: Predict

  • How many extra rooms should the hotel sell? There is no universally correct number. At the default settings (100 rooms, ADR ₹5,000, 60% guaranteed bookings, 15% non-guaranteed no-show rate and ₹8,000 walking cost), compare the net-contribution curve and select the overbooking level with the highest simulated value. The page marks it with ★.
  • How risky is walking? Use the displayed “Nights with a walked guest” percentage. Low is under 25%; medium is 25–60%; high is over 60%. The correct risk category depends on the selected overbooking level and current assumptions.
  • Why? Some reservations will not show. Overbooking can replace rooms likely to remain empty, but if too many guests arrive, the hotel must walk guests and pay compensation.

Model answers: Compare and Recommend

  • Scenario A vs B: Compare occupancy, RevPAR, net contribution, walk risk and average guests walked. Recommend the scenario with the stronger net contribution only after considering its walking risk and service impact. A higher occupancy rate alone does not prove a better decision.
  • Recommendation to the GM: A defensible answer is “Use a dynamic overbooking limit based on guaranteed booking share, no-show history, ADR and the current cost of walking. Choose the level with the highest simulated net contribution, but apply a service-risk limit and review it against actual hotel data.”
  • Conservative (0–3 extra rooms): Suitable when walking is very costly, the hotel is small, guest loyalty is important, or forecasts are uncertain. Risk is lower but empty rooms may remain.
  • Balanced (4–8 extra rooms): A reasonable starting range for testing, not a guaranteed optimum. Validate it with the model and actual no-show data.
  • Aggressive (9+ extra rooms): May offset many no-shows but can substantially increase walking risk. Use only if evidence supports it and a robust relocation plan exists.

Model answers: Reflect

  1. Which guest-cycle stage is damaged and who handles recovery? The Arrival stage is directly affected because a guest with a confirmed reservation cannot be accommodated. Reception/front office should coordinate immediately with the Front Office Manager and Guest Relations; reservations and duty management should arrange a suitable alternative hotel, transport if appropriate, clear communication and compensation according to policy.
  2. Why does guaranteed share change the best level? In this model, guaranteed bookings have a 4% no-show rate, while the non-guaranteed no-show rate is adjustable and defaults to 15%. A larger guaranteed share therefore generally reduces expected no-shows, so less overbooking may be needed. Guaranteed no-shows also pay one night’s rate in this simulation.
  3. Small hotel or airport/transit hotel? A small hotel should generally be more cautious because even one walked guest represents a larger share of its inventory and may harm reputation disproportionately. An airport/transit hotel may have volatile arrival patterns and time-sensitive guests; it should use reliable demand data, stronger confirmation practices and a clear relocation plan rather than applying a fixed overbooking number blindly.
  4. What might a prediction miss? It may overlook variation in arrivals, the different no-show rates for guaranteed and non-guaranteed bookings, the ₹ cost of walking, and the difference between occupancy and contribution. A better approach is to test multiple settings, record evidence, compare scenarios and acknowledge that the model excludes reputation, loyalty, room-type constraints and many real operating costs.

Important limitations to tell students

  • The page uses simulated nights, not a hotel’s actual historical data.
  • The model values each accommodated guest at the same ADR and does not model room types, overbooking of specific categories, upgrades or lost ancillary spend.
  • It treats guaranteed no-show charges as retained revenue and subtracts a fixed compensation cost for each walked guest.
  • It does not quantify reputation damage, loyalty loss, staff time, transport, legal obligations or all operating costs. Therefore “net contribution” here is a simplified model measure, not full accounting profit.

Teacher’s marking principle: Accept different recommendations when students support them with figures from their own simulation, explain the trade-off between revenue and walking risk, and acknowledge model limitations.
